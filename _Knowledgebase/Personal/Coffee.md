## The anatomy of a coffee cherry

Before processing makes sense, you need to know what you're working with. A coffee cherry has layers: skin → pulp (mucilage) → parchment → silver skin → the bean itself. Processing is just the question of _how much of that stuff you strip away, in what order, and how fast_.

---

## The three main processing methods

**1. Washed (wet process)**

The skin and all the fruit pulp are mechanically stripped off almost immediately after harvest, leaving beans still coated in a thin layer of mucilage. Those beans are then submerged in water tanks and left to ferment for 24–72 hours — the fermentation breaks down and loosens that remaining mucilage so it can be rinsed off cleanly. Then the beans dry.

**Flavor result:** Clean, bright, high-clarity. The terroir and variety of the bean come through because there's no fruit sugar or fermentation character muddying the signal. Think crisp acidity, defined fruit notes, transparent cup. Ethiopia Yirgacheffe washed is the canonical example — you get jasmine and bergamot, not raisin and chocolate.

---

**2. Natural (dry process)**

The whole cherry — skin, fruit, everything — is laid out to dry in the sun for 3–6 weeks. The bean sits inside fermenting fruit the entire time, slowly absorbing sugars and fermentation byproducts through the parchment.

**Flavor result:** Heavy, boozy, winey, tropical. The cup is thick and funky — strawberry jam, blueberry, dark chocolate. There's less clarity; it's a loud, complex profile. Ethiopia Sidama naturals, Ethiopian Harrar. The downside: high risk of defect if the drying isn't managed perfectly. Inconsistent fermentation = rotty, off-flavors.

---

**3. Honey process (semi-washed / pulped natural)**

The skin is removed mechanically, but varying amounts of mucilage are left on the bean before it goes to dry. The name reflects how sticky and amber-colored the mucilage looks. Sub-categories (yellow, red, black honey) refer to how much mucilage is left and how long they dry — black honey leaves almost everything on and dries slowly under shade; yellow honey leaves a thin layer and dries fast in open sun.

**Flavor result:** The middle ground. More body and sweetness than washed, more clarity than natural. Stone fruit, brown sugar, a bit of funk. Costa Rica and El Salvador producers made this famous.

---

## Fermentation: what's actually happening

Fermentation isn't a processing method — it's a _step inside_ each method, and increasingly a tool producers are deliberately manipulating.

In traditional washed processing, fermentation happens in the water tank to remove mucilage — it's functionally a means to an end. But the microorganisms doing that work (wild yeasts, bacteria) also produce acids, alcohols, and aromatic compounds that the bean absorbs. The longer it ferments and the warmer the environment, the more those compounds develop. Most producers target a clean, controlled ferment: 36–48 hours, not too warm, done.

**Anaerobic fermentation** is where things get weird and deliberate. Producers seal the whole cherries (or depulped beans) in oxygen-free tanks, controlling temperature, CO₂ levels, and duration precisely. Anaerobic conditions change which microbes dominate — you get lactic acid bacteria producing compounds like ethyl acetate that taste like tropical fruit, bubblegum, or fermented wine in ways that don't occur in open-air ferment. The result is often _very_ distinct — intentionally funky, almost perfume-like. It's divisive. Some people love it; others find it overpowering or artificial-tasting.

**Carbonic maceration** (borrowed from Burgundy winemaking) is a variant: whole cherries in CO₂ tanks, where intracellular fermentation occurs inside each intact cherry. Ultra-syrupy, rich, unusual flavor complexity.

|Method|Clarity|Body|Acidity|Typical notes|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Washed|High|Light–medium|Bright|Floral, citrus, tea-like|
|Honey|Medium|Medium|Balanced|Stone fruit, brown sugar|
|Natural|Low|Heavy|Soft|Berry jam, wine, chocolate|
|Anaerobic|Variable|Heavy|Complex|Tropical, bubblegum, boozy|

## Practical takeaway

If you're brewing light roast and want to taste the bean's origin clearly — go washed. If you want something loud and syrupy with the bag screaming "blueberry" at you — natural. Honey is the people-pleaser. Anaerobic is either a revelation or a gimmick depending on execution and your palate.

The four paths converge on the same green bean — but they've taken radically different routes to get there, and that's exactly what you taste in the cup.
