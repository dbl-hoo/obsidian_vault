# New York — one firm on both sides, different licensees per party

**Short answer:** 🚩 **RPL § 443 is residential-only.** § 443(2): *"This section shall apply only to transactions involving residential real property"* — defined in § 443(1)(f) as a 1–4 family dwelling, condo, or co-op improved with a residence (not vacant land, not commercial). Everything below about the statutory dual-agency-with-designated-sales-agents regime applies to **residential deals**. For KBC's typical commercial/industrial deals, § 443's disclosure form and consent mechanics don't apply by their own terms — I did not find a parallel NY statute imposing an equivalent disclosure/consent regime for commercial transactions. Commercial dual agency in NY appears to run on common-law agency/fiduciary-duty principles rather than a codified disclosure form. **Confirm with local counsel before relying on this for a commercial deal** — this file is not verified for that fact pattern.

*Below (residential): Unlike Illinois, New York does **not** have a separate "designated agency" regime that avoids dual agency when different licensees handle each side. Under RPL § 443(3)(f), that setup is still legally **dual agency** — the brokerage/supervising broker is the dual agent — just executed through "dual agency with designated sales agents." It requires full written informed consent from **both** principals (the owner/landlord and the tenant/buyer), using the statutory disclosure form, even though Licensee A only works with the owner and Licensee B only works with the tenant.*

## The regime

**There's only one track: dual agency (RPL § 443)**
- No NY equivalent to Illinois's 225 ILCS 454/15-50, which treats different-licensee-per-side as *not* dual agency at all. In NY, the firm is the dual agent regardless of how many individual licensees are staffed on the deal.
- "Designated sales agent" = a salesperson/associate broker "assigned to represent a client when a different client is also represented by such real estate broker in the same transaction" (RPL § 443(1)). That's Licensee A/Licensee B in your fact pattern.
- With both principals' informed written consent, the dual-agent broker may designate Licensee A to represent the owner and Licensee B to represent the tenant, each negotiating only for their assigned side.
- Even so, **neither designated agent can provide the full range of fiduciary duties** — the statutory form requires disclosing that, like the dual agent supervising them, they "cannot provide undivided loyalty."

**Disclosure mechanics**
- Statutory form (RPL § 443(4)) must be provided and acknowledged:
  - Listing/landlord's agent → to owner/landlord, before the listing agreement is signed.
  - Seller's/landlord's agent → to buyer/tenant (or their agent) at first substantive contact.
- Signed acknowledgments must be retained **3 years**.
- Advance consent to dual agency (and to dual agency with designated sales agents specifically) can be baked into the form itself (RPL § 443(3)(f)) — but it's still consent to dual agency, not a workaround from it.

**No confidentiality-screen carve-out found.** Unlike Illinois's ownership-interest bar, NY's statute text doesn't contain a provision barring designation based on an agent having received the other side's confidential information. Don't assume a screen fixes anything here that consent doesn't already have to cover.

## Practical checklist (firm on both sides of a NY deal)

1. Treat it as dual agency from the start — there's no lesser designated-agency path to fall back on.
2. Get written informed consent from **both** the owner/landlord and the tenant/buyer on the statutory RPL § 443(4) form, specifically consenting to "dual agency with designated sales agents" (not just generic dual agency) if that's the structure.
3. Confirm the form names Licensee A and Licensee B as the respective designated agents.
4. Retain signed acknowledgments 3 years.
5. Remember the fiduciary-duty ceiling applies to both designated agents individually, not just the supervising broker — brief both licensees accordingly.
