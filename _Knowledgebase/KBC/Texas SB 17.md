## Texas SB 17 — Summary

**Effective:** September 1, 2025 | **Codified:** Texas Property Code, Chapter 5, Subchapter H

**The core rule:** Prohibits the acquisition of any interest in real property in Texas by individuals, entities, or governments associated with countries designated as national security risks — initially China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea.

**Who's covered on the country side:**

- Countries identified by the U.S. Director of National Intelligence as posing a national security risk, plus any additional countries the Texas Governor designates.

**Who's a prohibited "entity":**

- The definition of "companies" explicitly includes corporations, partnerships, LLCs — _and_ wholly owned subsidiaries, majority-owned subsidiaries, parent companies, and affiliates of those entities that exist to make a profit.

**Penalties:**

- Civil penalties up to the greater of $250,000 or 50% of the property's market value; individuals who knowingly violate it face state jail felony charges; the AG can pursue forced divestiture.

**Leases vs. purchases — critical distinction:**

- A purchase or acquisition that violates SB 17 is _not_ automatically void — but a leasehold interest of one year or more acquired in violation of the Act _is_ void and unenforceable.

**Litigation:** The Chinese American Legal Defense Alliance filed a federal class action challenging SB 17 as violating equal protection, the Supremacy Clause, and the Fair Housing Act. The case is ongoing; enforcement continues in the meantime.

## Impact on a US Subsidiary Wholly Owned by a Chinese-Listed Parent

This is where your client's situation gets genuinely ugly, and there's no clean workaround.

**The subsidiary doesn't save you.** SB 17's definition of "companies" expressly includes wholly owned subsidiaries, majority-owned subsidiaries, parent companies, and affiliates — so the US LLC or corporation entity form provides no shelter if the prohibited ownership traces back up the chain.

**Layered structures won't work either.** Creative structuring through layered entities or trusts will not circumvent the law if control or ownership ultimately traces back to a prohibited party.

**Leases are the worst outcome.** Unlike a purchase (which is merely subject to enforcement/divestiture), leases of one year or more acquired by prohibited parties on or after September 1, 2025, are void and unenforceable — and renewals or extensions may constitute new "acquisitions" requiring updated compliance review.

**The "traded on a Chinese exchange" wrinkle:** This is a gray area the statute doesn't cleanly resolve. If the parent company is incorporated and headquartered in China, it's squarely in scope. If the parent is a Cayman or BVI holdco that _lists_ on a Chinese exchange (common structure), the analysis depends on whether it's "domiciled in" or "controlled by" a designated country — which is a facts-and-circumstances inquiry the AG's rulemaking hasn't fully addressed yet.

## Bottom Line / Trade-offs

|Scenario|Risk Level|
|---|---|
|Parent HQ'd in China, US sub signs TX lease ≥1 yr|**Void lease. High criminal/civil exposure.**|
|Cayman/BVI holdco listed on Chinese exchange, US sub|**Gray — depends on control/domicile facts**|
|Lease term < 1 year|**Not void**, but still subject to AG enforcement action|
|Pre-Sept. 1, 2025 lease|**Grandfathered under prior law** (may be held/managed)|

**Practical advice for any deal involving this structure:**

1. Get a Texas-qualified outside counsel opinion on whether the parent's domicile/control facts trigger the statute before the client touches anything.
2. Don't assume the US sub's domestic formation is a firewall — SB 17 expressly blew that up.
3. Watch the AG rulemaking and the CALDA litigation — both could clarify (or invalidate) material provisions. But don't plan around invalidation.

Not legal advice, obviously, but you've got enough here to brief the deal team and flag this as a hard stop pending proper TX counsel review.
